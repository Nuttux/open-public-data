# Open questions for the next rubric version

## Settled in v4 — kept here so they are not re-opened

Anchored to numbers after eight readers were measured disagreeing by up to 23
points: street levels, roadway acceptances, contractor time extensions,
one-address permits, subdivision maps, set-backs, sidewalks, land bought with
and without a stated purpose, hearings, index pages, no-subject lines.

Also settled: repeals take the score of the act they undo; a matter taken up is
the act at a proposal's discount; a fact the Board records but does not act on
takes its own reach less a third; a department's office lease is 26 while a
lease of space the public uses is scored on what it houses; an appointment is
24; an environmental finding that gates an act is 22; and the discount ladder
has three rungs, because readers needed a middle one and invented it.

The recurring utility bill was a CONTRADICTION, not a gap — named in two
sections at once, worth 55-62 in one and about 36 in the other. It is upkeep.

## Cross-band — these DO meet the bar for v6

**Disposing of city property at one address lands in three bands.** One reader
scored access rights sold to a neighbour 24, a city lot leased at auction 48,
and land and building sold 55. Three sections each claim it: "a public asset
given away" is 55-79, "reach picks the band" makes one lot 30-54, and 10-29
explicitly owns "the city forgoes and one party benefits". One shape, three
answers.

**A lease that keeps a public-safety facility running.** The lease anchor says
score public-use space on what it houses — a fire station, 68. The recurring-bill
rule says a monthly rent on a building already standing is upkeep, about 30. The
same reader reached three different rules for three neighbouring records: a fire
station lease 30, court quarters 60, land for the public morgue 50.

**The proposal-discount fork decides more than any anchor.** x0.75 against x0.5
is worth 20+ points on a large subject and nothing in the line picks it.
"Congress urged to ratify the city's reservoir rights of way" took 68; "W. J.
Cuthbertson suggests a city hall at Market and Hermann" took 41. A stated test
for the middle rung would remove more spread than any single new anchor.

**Civic buildings the table does not name.** School, hospital and library are
78; fire, police and engine house 68. An administrative or operational building
— a city office block, a coroner's morgue, a municipal asphalt plant, a
corporation yard — has nothing, and one reader interpolated 48 to 60.

**A transit line itself has no anchor.** Land for one is pinned at 62; the line
is not. A reader met the Geary Street Municipal Railway being planned, declared
necessary, ordered and extended — 25 lines — and invented 82 and 78. Those
scores were the only reason anything in its batch reached the top band.

**"Finish the whole thing" against the part-and-whole rule.** "$90,000 set aside
to finish the Polytechnic High School academic building" names a whole facility
and is plainly an instalment: 78 by one reading, 45 by the other, a 33-point
swing. And is "the academic building" a facility or a named part of a school?

**A bond election is not machinery.** "Voters to decide five borrowing
questions", "$3.5M for municipal streetcars", "$34.5M for Spring Valley Water".
The 30-54 band calls a financing instrument machinery, but a citywide vote on
acquiring the water supply is not an instrument. A reader used 65-78 with no
anchor to lean on.

**The component anchor has no scale.** The same 45 went to "$25,000 for
engineering on the Hetch Hetchy project" — a plan for the city's whole water
supply — and "$305 for pipes and doors at Polytechnic High School". 73 records
took it in one batch.

**A purchase for a facility already open.** The component anchor says "for a
building under construction", so sterilizers for the hospital, cot covers for
the jails and linoleum in City Hall fall between it and the maintenance rule.
One reader invented three numbers — 45 for fixed plant, 30 for consumables, 24
for the city furnishing its own offices. ~25 lines a batch.

**The lease contradiction, unfixed for leases.** Two readers now. "Whitcomb
Estate paid $1,050 rent for hospitals on Stevenson Street" is a public-use lease
scored on what it houses (high) and a recurring rent on a standing building
(upkeep, ~30). This is exactly the hydrant-water contradiction, which was fixed
for utilities and not for this.

## For v6 — held back deliberately

**The major-thoroughfare rung is inverted and never fires.** 40 requires a
listed street AND a named length, so "Street levels to be changed along the
Great Highway" scores 34 while a named stretch of that highway would score 40.
The whole ranks below its own part. A reader found all 148 street-level lines in
its batch landing on 34, the 40 rung dead. The fix is to drop the length
requirement.

NOT fixed now, on a rule worth stating: **bump when a case moves records across

## The permit anchor has no discount floor — crosses a band

anchor and stops there. So "Chas. Roof asks to set off blasts" halves the 33
permit anchor to 17 while "Chas. Roof allowed to set off blasts" stays 33 — the

## The thoroughfare split exists for levels and not for street WORK

## The suffix rule inverts Third — a rule error, not a judgement

## THE traffic hole, stated precisely — a cross-band contradiction in v6

The anchor table pins "a street or part of one closed" at 36. The 55-79 band
lists "a street's use changed in a way residents would feel". A permanent
closure is the most drastic use change there is, and it sits a full band below
where the band text puts it.

That contradiction decides every traffic record: truck bans, bike lanes,
transit-only lanes, one-way conversions, midday car bans, permit-parking
districts, stop signs, meters. One reader measured ~150 records in a single

roughly DOUBLE that batch's 55-79 share.

Populations reported across readers: 21, 24, 28, 30, 39, 54, 72, 76, 79, 90, 97,
104, 109 records a batch. It is the largest hole in the rubric by a wide margin
and the only one that is also a contradiction rather than an absence.

Every reader invented a ladder. The invented values ranged 26 to 62 for the same
shapes.

## The appeal cycle erases its own subject — 164 records, 14% of a modern batch

One contested permit generates six to ten near-identical clerk lines: appeal
heard, vote sought to uphold, vote sought to overturn, certification put off,
findings sought, motion tabled. Two anchors absorb the whole population flat —
hearing 16, environmental finding 22 — so 161 of 164 land in 10-29 and the size
of the underlying subject never reaches the score:

    Appeal heard over 203 low-income flats at Pierce and Scott Streets   16
    Appeal heard over gym locker rooms at 1318-1320 7th Avenue           16

This is most of why modern batches run 54% in 10-29 against the corpus's 22%. It
is the modern-era twin of the traffic hole: the historic decades lose street-use
rules to a contradiction, the modern decades lose land-use decisions to a flat
anchor.

The fix is probably that a hearing or finding inherits a fraction of the act it
gates, rather than a flat number — which is what the discount ladder already
does everywhere else.

## RESOLVED as a diagnosis: one ambiguous sentence, not three defects

I logged the permit tier as "three readers point at three places, a fix from any
one alone would be a guess". Batch 037's reader found why. The sentence

    "Below 30, an anchor is not discounted"

has two readings, and it never says which:

  (a) an anchor whose VALUE is below 30 is not discounted — written for the
      18-day extension, and the reading that halves the 33 permit to 17
  (b) a discount may not LAND below 30 — which floors the permit request at 30
      and the refused subdivision map at 30

Every symptom is downstream of that. Reading (a) produces the 33/17 split on
"allowed to" vs "asks to"; reading (a) produces the subdivision-map cliff (36
refused -> 18); reading (b) is what the reader who defended 17 was arguing
against. Three readers, three symptoms, one sentence.

So it is one edit, not a guess — but WHICH reading is a judgement about the
preference model, not something the corpus can settle:

    reading (a) says asking is genuinely a lesser act than being allowed,
      and a request for a permit belongs in the band below
    reading (b) says a decision about one address stays a decision about one
      address whichever way it goes, and only the precision changes

(a) is closer to what one reader defended out loud ("seeks a spur track" = 17).
(b) is closer to the housing-bond and appeal fixes in v7, which both say the
stage must not swallow the subject. v7 cannot ship until this is picked, because
entries 9 and 10 lean on (b) and the permit anchor currently behaves as (a).

## MEASURED: the permit anchor was calibrated on grants, not applications

Batch 039's reader argued that halving a permit application double-counts,
because "the 33 anchor was itself measured on Journal lines that are
overwhelmingly applications". Measured across all 6,818 permit-kind lines in the
corpus (garage, boiler, oil tank, filling station, laundry, blasting, spur):

    phrased as a GRANT          4,953    72.6%
    neither                     1,363    20.0%
    phrased as an APPLICATION     263     3.9%
    phrased as a REFUSAL          239     3.5%

The claim is false. The anchor was calibrated on grant-phrased lines, so
discounting an application below it is the ladder working, not double-counting.
That is evidence for reading (a).

Two consequences, and the second is the bigger one:

**The permit-application question is small.** The reader called it "a large share
of the pre-1930 lines"; it is 263 records corpus-wide, plus 239 refusals. That is
below the bar that the appeal cycle (164 a BATCH) and the unanchored ladders
(41-258 a batch) clear comfortably. It is not a v7 blocker; it is a footnote.

**And it never blocked v7 in the first place — my error.** I wrote that entries 9
and 10 lean on reading (b) and so v7 could not ship until the reading was picked.
That conflated two different things. Reading (a) says a stage DISCOUNTS an act;
entry 9 says a stage must not REPLACE the act's subject. "Appeal heard over 203
low-income flats" scoring a flat 16 is not a discount — it is the subject being
thrown away. Discount the stage, keep the subject: both hold at once, under
either reading.

So v7 ships without settling the permit reading, and keeps the current behaviour
(a), which is now the measured one.

The subdivision-map refusal cliff is a separate thing and stays open — it comes
from "a refusal above the permit tier takes the act at x0.5" meeting an anchor 3
points above the tier, not from the ambiguous floor sentence.

## The discount floor is wrong at a third value, which settles its shape

Three concrete incoherences now, all from the same clause:

    a 33 permit asked for      -> 17, BELOW the 18 anchor the floor protects
    a 30 sidewalk-tables permit -> 15, below a 22 event permit left at 22
    a 36 subdivision map refused -> 18

The pattern is monotonicity: after discounting, a bigger act scores below a
smaller one. Patching the floor's threshold cannot fix that, because the
"anchors below 30 are never discounted" rule is what creates the inversion — it
protects the small anchors and exposes the large ones.

    REPLACE both clauses with one:
      "A discount never takes an act below 18. Anchors are discounted like
       anything else; the floor catches them."

Then a requested event permit, a requested sidewalk-tables permit and a requested
blasting permit all land at 18. That is honest — each is a request for a small
thing at one address — and no act can be discounted beneath a smaller one.
