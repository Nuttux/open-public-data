# Open questions for the next rubric version

## Settled in v4 — kept here so they are not re-opened

Anchored to numbers after eight readers were measured disagreeing by up to 23
points: street levels, roadway acceptances, contractor time extensions,
one-address permits, subdivision maps, set-backs, sidewalks, land bought with
and without a stated purpose, hearings, index pages, no-subject lines.

Also settled: repeals take the score of the act they undo; a matter taken up is
the act at a proposal's discount; a fact the Board records but does not act on
takes its own reach less a third; a department's office lease is 26 while a
lease of space the public uses is scored on what it houses; an appointment is
24; an environmental finding that gates an act is 22; and the discount ladder
has three rungs, because readers needed a middle one and invented it.

The recurring utility bill was a CONTRADICTION, not a gap — named in two
sections at once, worth 55-62 in one and about 36 in the other. It is upkeep.

## Cross-band — these DO meet the bar for v6

**Disposing of city property at one address lands in three bands.** One reader
scored access rights sold to a neighbour 24, a city lot leased at auction 48,
and land and building sold 55. Three sections each claim it: "a public asset
given away" is 55-79, "reach picks the band" makes one lot 30-54, and 10-29
explicitly owns "the city forgoes and one party benefits". One shape, three
answers.

**A lease that keeps a public-safety facility running.** The lease anchor says
score public-use space on what it houses — a fire station, 68. The recurring-bill
rule says a monthly rent on a building already standing is upkeep, about 30. The
same reader reached three different rules for three neighbouring records: a fire
station lease 30, court quarters 60, land for the public morgue 50.

**The proposal-discount fork decides more than any anchor.** x0.75 against x0.5
is worth 20+ points on a large subject and nothing in the line picks it.
"Congress urged to ratify the city's reservoir rights of way" took 68; "W. J.
Cuthbertson suggests a city hall at Market and Hermann" took 41. A stated test
for the middle rung would remove more spread than any single new anchor.

**Civic buildings the table does not name.** School, hospital and library are
78; fire, police and engine house 68. An administrative or operational building
— a city office block, a coroner's morgue, a municipal asphalt plant, a
corporation yard — has nothing, and one reader interpolated 48 to 60.

**A transit line itself has no anchor.** Land for one is pinned at 62; the line
is not. A reader met the Geary Street Municipal Railway being planned, declared
necessary, ordered and extended — 25 lines — and invented 82 and 78. Those
scores were the only reason anything in its batch reached the top band.

**"Finish the whole thing" against the part-and-whole rule.** "$90,000 set aside
to finish the Polytechnic High School academic building" names a whole facility
and is plainly an instalment: 78 by one reading, 45 by the other, a 33-point
swing. And is "the academic building" a facility or a named part of a school?

**A bond election is not machinery.** "Voters to decide five borrowing
questions", "$3.5M for municipal streetcars", "$34.5M for Spring Valley Water".
The 30-54 band calls a financing instrument machinery, but a citywide vote on
acquiring the water supply is not an instrument. A reader used 65-78 with no
anchor to lean on.

**The component anchor has no scale.** The same 45 went to "$25,000 for
engineering on the Hetch Hetchy project" — a plan for the city's whole water
supply — and "$305 for pipes and doors at Polytechnic High School". 73 records
took it in one batch.

**A purchase for a facility already open.** The component anchor says "for a
building under construction", so sterilizers for the hospital, cot covers for
the jails and linoleum in City Hall fall between it and the maintenance rule.
One reader invented three numbers — 45 for fixed plant, 30 for consumables, 24
for the city furnishing its own offices. ~25 lines a batch.

**The lease contradiction, unfixed for leases.** Two readers now. "Whitcomb
Estate paid $1,050 rent for hospitals on Stevenson Street" is a public-use lease
scored on what it houses (high) and a recurring rent on a standing building
(upkeep, ~30). This is exactly the hydrant-water contradiction, which was fixed
for utilities and not for this.

## Confirmed dead by three readers

The major-thoroughfare rung fires on 0.3% of street-level lines, or not at all.
Two readers reported it never firing; a third saw it fire four times in 1,200.
Whatever it was meant to lift, it does not. Fix with the inversion above, or
drop the rung and let every street-level record take 34.

Also mine, and sloppy: the street list carries no suffixes. Nineteenth Avenue is
a thoroughfare; Nineteenth Street is a Potrero side street. Third and Ocean have
the same trap.

## Closed by v5, confirmed by a reader

"A street closed for an afternoon" and "land bought to widen a street" both
landed on their new anchors without argument — 22 and 48 — in the batch that
had reported them.

## For v6 — held back deliberately

**The major-thoroughfare rung is inverted and never fires.** 40 requires a
listed street AND a named length, so "Street levels to be changed along the
Great Highway" scores 34 while a named stretch of that highway would score 40.
The whole ranks below its own part. A reader found all 148 street-level lines in
its batch landing on 34, the 40 rung dead. The fix is to drop the length
requirement.

NOT fixed now, on a rule worth stating: **bump when a case moves records across
a band; do not bump when it moves them within one.** This is 6 points inside
30-54. The logic is wrong, the result is coherent — every street-level record
lands on the same number — and a fourth re-read of 99,278 lines does not buy
enough to be worth it.

Also waiting for v6, same test applied:
* a condition attached to a permit — a blasting curfew, a required flagman
* a system installed in a building already standing, where the line does not
  say whether the building is going up
* a repeal that names an ordinance number and no act
* a grant of public space longer than a night and shorter than a permit
* boulevard designation

## Still open

**Building a civic thing in instalments.** Four readers, four resolutions: a sum
ladder, building-wide-system-counts-as-the-building, a facility ladder, and
whole-facility-versus-component. The last is the most defensible — "E. W.
Elliott paid for Engine House No. 41" is the engine house, "Ralston Iron Works
paid $5,150 for steel at Hancock Grammar School" is money at a site — but it is
one reader's invention, not a measured answer.

**The same purchase written twice, once with its purpose.** MEASURED: 193
groups, 0.19% of lines, and the v4 anchors narrow the gap from 38 points to 26.
Not closed: a reader seeing both spellings on adjacent lines still has to score
them differently, and "judge only what the line states" is what makes it so.

**A land-use rule at one-street reach.** A set-back is anchored at 40, but the
30-54 band's list holds nothing shaped like a rule — one address, money at a
site, a business permit, a subdivision map. The number is pinned; the reasoning
under it is not.

**Land bought to widen a street. CLOSED by v6** — anchored at 48 as "land
bought for a public work". This entry said 44 and a reader followed v6 instead
and then had to correct its own earlier lines. Two files pointing different
ways is worse than either number.

**How wide is "civic"?** The land anchor names a school, a hospital, a
playground. A reservoir, a pumping station, a fire drill tower and a municipal
parking garage are none of those. One reader put the first three at 70 and
parking land at 50, a line it invented.

**A repeal whose underlying act is not in the line.** "Sand removal order
dropped on Balboa Street" — v4 says score the act undone, which requires knowing
what a sand-removal order is worth.

**A street closed for an afternoon.** A soap-box derby, an Easter egg hunt, a
summer play street. A real grant of public space, time-boxed, and no band covers
it.

## A process fault, not a rubric one

A reader noticed IMPORTANCE.md changing under it — sha 63ebd0b93a to e82ed24d0e
between two of its own commands. Stamping the rubric at hand-out keeps the
ACCOUNTING correct, but a worker still read a moving file. Do not edit the
rubric while batches are out; bump the version, let the wave land, then change
it.

## Answered outside the rubric

**A proposal and its adoption, both in the same volume.** One reader asked
whether the half-discount double-counts a fact the Journal simply prints twice.
It does — and the fix is not in the scoring. The duplicate-collapsing pass keeps
ONE row per act carrying its final outcome, so the proposal row does not reach
a reader at all. 17% of a batch is this duplication.

## v6 contradicts itself on the thoroughfare rung — mine, within-band

"Four fixes to anchors that were wrong" says: drop the length requirement, a
listed street as the subject is 40. "Which streets are major" still says: 40
fires only when a listed street is the SUBJECT and a length is named. I wrote
both. A reader followed the fix, and it still fired 4 times in 113 street-level
lines; under the other reading it fires zero.

NOT fixed while a wave is out, and it fails the bump test anyway — 34 against
40 is 6 points inside one band. Delete the second sentence whenever the rubric
is next opened.

## The permit anchor has no discount floor — crosses a band

"Below 30, an anchor is not discounted" was written to protect the 18 extension
anchor and stops there. So "Chas. Roof asks to set off blasts" halves the 33
permit anchor to 17 while "Chas. Roof allowed to set off blasts" stays 33 — the
same permit, both spellings a few lines apart in one batch, 16 points and a band
apart. ~14 records a batch.

The floor should be the anchor itself for any anchored permit-tier act, which is
what the extension case already says in different words. Fix in the same pass as
the thoroughfare contradiction (two readers have now reported that one).

## The thoroughfare split exists for levels and not for street WORK

A reader measured it: 151 street-level lines in its batch, against 292
paving/grading/sewering/sidewalk lines — four times as many, and 218 of them
landed on the flat 38. Fifty-two name a listed thoroughfare. "Mission Street to
be repaved from Fourth to the Embarcadero" scores the same as a fifty-foot patch
on an alley.

The street-level anchor got a 34/40 split; the street-work anchor, which is the
larger population, got none.

## The citywide-component multiplier has no floor

v6 added "a component of a CITYWIDE system takes the system's score less a
third" without a lower bound, so "$36 more toward a Heisler locomotive" takes 53
while "$29,311.05 for San Francisco Hospital brickwork" takes the flat component
45. Any trivial material for a citywide system outranks a large trade on a civic
building. ~46 records a batch in the Hetch Hetchy and Municipal Railway years.

## The suffix rule inverts Third — a rule error, not a judgement

v6 says "read the list as avenues where the city has one". That makes Third
AVENUE — a Richmond side street — the thoroughfare, and demotes Third STREET,
which is the actual arterial and the subject of lines like "Third Street rezoned
from factories to neighbourhood shops". Ocean and Nineteenth come out right
under the rule; Third comes out backwards and must be listed as a street.

## The three populations with no anchor at all — measured per batch

These are now the largest holes. Every reader invents a private ladder, and the
ladders do not match.

**Traffic and parking regulation — 28 to 97 records a batch, in every modern
year.** Stop signs, meters, tow-away zones, turn bans, one-way conversions,
speed limits, bike lanes, transit-only lanes, permit-parking districts. Two
sections claim them at different bands: "a street's use changed in a way
residents would feel" (55-79) and one-street reach (30-54). Ladders invented so
far ranged 30 to 58 for the same shapes.

**A lease of space the public uses — up to 117 records a batch in the modern
years.** The department-office anchor (26) is clean; "score it on what it
houses" gives no number, so readers invented clinic 65, branch library 70,
courtrooms 55, and 26 for anything the clerk called an office. A 44-point spread
decided by one word.

**Landmark and historic designation — 13 to 44 records a batch.** A permanent
rule on what may be done to one building. Not a permit, not a set-back, not a
subdivision map; the 30-54 band's list holds nothing rule-shaped. Readers used
40 to 55, and the proposal discount puts a granted designation and a proposed
one in different bands.

## A line naming a subject but no act — 17 records a batch

"Board adopts Resolution No. 571-10 for the Transbay Transit Center." The
subject is named and large; the act is not stated. Scoring it as the neighbouring
act gives 48, scoring it as procedure gives 8 — five bands apart, and the shape
appears in every modern batch.

## MEASURED: how much two readers agree

An accidental double-dispatch scored three batches twice — same input, same
rubric, independent readers:

    batch 018   87% identical   97% same band   mean |diff| 0.6 points
    batch 019   70% identical   93% same band   mean |diff| 1.5 points
    batch 020   65% identical   94% same band   mean |diff| 1.6 points
    batch 025   61% identical   96% same band   Pearson r 0.969, 88% within 5

Two readers put a record in the same band 93-97% of the time and differ by
about 1.5 points out of 100. That is the reliability the six rubric versions and
four re-reads bought, and it is smaller than the width of any band.

One reader reported the same pair as "differs on 865 of 1,200 rows", which is
true and useless: it counts a one-point difference the same as a two-band one.

## THE traffic hole, stated precisely — a cross-band contradiction in v6

The anchor table pins "a street or part of one closed" at 36. The 55-79 band
lists "a street's use changed in a way residents would feel". A permanent
closure is the most drastic use change there is, and it sits a full band below
where the band text puts it.

That contradiction decides every traffic record: truck bans, bike lanes,
transit-only lanes, one-way conversions, midday car bans, permit-parking
districts, stop signs, meters. One reader measured ~150 records in a single
batch turning on it, and following the band text instead of the anchor would
roughly DOUBLE that batch's 55-79 share.

Populations reported across readers: 21, 24, 28, 30, 39, 54, 72, 76, 79, 90, 97,
104, 109 records a batch. It is the largest hole in the rubric by a wide margin
and the only one that is also a contradiction rather than an absence.

Every reader invented a ladder. The invented values ranged 26 to 62 for the same
shapes.

## The appeal cycle erases its own subject — 164 records, 14% of a modern batch

One contested permit generates six to ten near-identical clerk lines: appeal
heard, vote sought to uphold, vote sought to overturn, certification put off,
findings sought, motion tabled. Two anchors absorb the whole population flat —
hearing 16, environmental finding 22 — so 161 of 164 land in 10-29 and the size
of the underlying subject never reaches the score:

    Appeal heard over 203 low-income flats at Pierce and Scott Streets   16
    Appeal heard over gym locker rooms at 1318-1320 7th Avenue           16

This is most of why modern batches run 54% in 10-29 against the corpus's 22%. It
is the modern-era twin of the traffic hole: the historic decades lose street-use
rules to a contradiction, the modern decades lose land-use decisions to a flat
anchor.

The fix is probably that a hearing or finding inherits a fraction of the act it
gates, rather than a flat number — which is what the discount ladder already
does everywhere else.

## The housing-bond fork — 33 points on the clerk's wording

    $71M in housing bonds proposed for Mason and Eddy Streets        18
    $75M in bonds proposed for 137 flats at 4840 Mission Street      51

The machinery discount fires only when no unit count is written, so the same act
scores 33 points apart on whether the clerk mentioned how many flats. This is the
land-purchase shape (26 points, already logged) with a bigger gap and a bigger
population — ~20 records a batch in the bond years.

## The no-discount floor covers anchors only

"Below 30, an anchor is not discounted" is written for anchored acts. Every
non-anchored small act — a settlement, an advisory body, a proclamation, a
motion inside an appeal — has no floor at all, so the discount ladder drives it
toward the 0-9 band reserved for procedure with no subject. One reader refused
to let a proposed settlement fall below 10 and said so.

Same fault as the permit-floor item. One clause should fix both: no act with a
named subject falls into 0-9.

## WHERE the readers disagree — it is not random

A fifth double-read (batch 017): r 0.930, 92% same band, 83% within 5 points.
The useful part is the tail. Only 21 of 1,200 rows differ by 20 or more, and
they fall into three shapes, all already on this list:

    public-facing leases with the use unstated    4 records, 42 points apart
    housing bonds with no unit count              8 records, 33 points apart
    citywide capital-repair programmes            4 records, 26-34 points apart

So the residual disagreement is structural, not noise. Closing those entries
raises agreement directly rather than asymptotically — which is the argument for
v7 being worth one more re-read, and for stopping after it.

Five double-reads now: r 0.930 to 0.969, same band 92% to 97%.

## Two readers invented ladders independently and landed in the SAME BANDS

Batch 035 and batch 043 each stated a full ladder for the three unanchored
families before scoring, with no rubric guidance and no knowledge of each other.
The numbers differ. The bands do not:

    traffic / parking            035    043    same band?
      one street or block         38     34      yes  (30-54)
      a corridor / use changed    44     48      yes  (30-54)
      district-wide or citywide   58     62      yes  (55-79)

    public-use lease
      neighbourhood service       55     60      yes  (55-79)
      department office           26     26      yes  (10-29)

    landmark
      one building                42     44      yes  (30-54)
      a district or group         62     58      yes  (55-79)

Every comparable rung agrees on band; the largest gap on any of them is 5 points.

This changes what the missing anchors cost. The absence is losing PRECISION, not
correctness — readers left to themselves reach the same judgement and write a
different number for it. It also says the v7 ladders are close to right before
anyone has tested them, since two readers converged on them from scratch.

It does NOT explain the 42-point lease disagreement in the 017 double-read. That
gap is between a reader who scored the space by what it houses and one who scored
it as an office because the clerk wrote "office" — the two readers here both
scored by what it houses, so they never met the fork. The v7 lease rule has to
say which reading wins, and the ladder alone will not do it.
