# How much a decision matters

One number per record, 0–100, read from the written line and its record.

This is a RUBRIC, not a pattern. Four rounds of ranking established that the
thing being judged cannot be matched lexically, and one attempt to try anyway
scored WORSE than the formula it replaced — −0.112 against +0.229 on the round
where money was held out. The reason is always the same shape:

    a nurse-staffing emergency at San Francisco General   ranked 2nd of 18
    a dog hospital at 3200 California Street              ranked 10th of 16
    a radio contract at the veterans hospital             ranked 13th of 16

Every one of those says *hospital*. What separates them is how much of the city
they touch, and that has to be read.

## Reach picks the band. The lists only say what fits inside it.

Read this before the bands. Every band below names KINDS of decision, and a
reader applying those lists literally puts all four of these at 80:

    Height limits lowered across two areas around Powell and Bush Streets
    Homewood Terrace rezoned for shops and two-family housing
    Shops proposed on Judah Street between Tenth and Twenty-fifth avenues
    Buildings on Bay Shore Boulevard must sit ten feet back

All four are "a rule about what may be built where people live". They are not
the same size, and the band headline — *a lot of people at once* — is what
decides. Choose the band by how far the decision reaches, THEN check the band's
list to see whether this kind of thing belongs at that reach.

## The scale

**80–100 — the city changes for a lot of people at once.**
A rule about what may be built or banned where people live. Money into
something people use daily and would notice the loss of: a hospital, a library,
a school, a clinic, transit, a shelter. A benefit or obligation applying to a
whole class of people. A public asset gained.

**55–79 — a real change, for a neighbourhood or a defined group.**
Housing built or funded, with people housed at the end of it. A street's use
changed in a way residents would feel. A public asset given away — real, but it
is a loss, and it ranks below the same asset gained.

**30–54 — one address, one building, one block.**
Money at a site with no stated beneficiary. A permit for a business. A
subdivision map. A financing instrument attached to an address: a bond or a
note is machinery, and it scores below the same money described as housing
people.

**10–29 — one party's business, or a gesture.**
A settlement, a tax refund, an assessment appeal, a claim: the city pays or
forgoes, and one party benefits. A renaming, unless the name is one the city
knows. A commendation or a proclaimed week.

BUYING IS NOT SETTLING. "Robert Trost paid $13,696.50 for building Galileo
High School" is a payment to one party, and it is also a school existing. The
city discharging a liability belongs here; the city buying something that gets
built belongs with the thing bought. Score the school, not the cheque.

NON-BINDING IS NOT THE SAME AS SYMBOLIC. "Police asked why welfare mothers were
arrested, charges urged dropped" binds nobody, but it names a class of people
and seeks relief for them. That is the 55–79 band with the proposal discount
applied — not a gesture. A gesture is a commendation, a proclaimed week, a
renaming: something whose whole content is the saying of it.

**0–9 — procedure with no subject.**
A file severed, rules suspended, a matter tabled with the matter unnamed.
A page carrying only a verdict and a roll call.

## Three adjustments, in the reader's own words

**Adopted weighs much more than proposed** — but stage discounts, it does not
kill. *"a proposal would rank high if it's something huge or out of the
ordinary… a large or significant change."* So a proposal that would change the
city noticeably keeps most of its score; a routine proposal loses roughly half.

**Money into daily-life infrastructure outranks a larger sum on a financing
instrument.** *"when money goes to like a hospital, some library, basically an
infrastructure of the city that matters daily life, I rank it higher than a
similar or a little higher amount on like a housing bond."*

**Housing and shelter sit near the top** — but *housing people* and *a housing
bond at an address* are not the same thing, and only the first belongs there.

## What the money is worth

A sum raises the score inside its band; it does not move a record between
bands. $67.3M to buy one warehouse is still one address. $3.7M to house
homeless young people reaches people. The reader ranked the second above the
first, and this is the rule that says why.

AND IT DOES NOT LIFT MAINTENANCE. The rule exists so a small human-facing sum
is not buried, not so that any spending on a civic building is top-band. "$325
for a new roof on the Relief Home's Section J" is upkeep on one wing: the
facility's importance does not transfer to its roof. Repair, replacement and
routine upkeep are scored on what is being repaired — one wing, one boiler, one
roof — not on the institution around it.

## The line's grammar is not its stage

MEASURED, because two readers got this wrong on the same day and it would have
demoted 8,120 records. A line saying "to be" is NOT a proposal:

    lines containing "to be"     23.3% sit over a record that says adopted,
                                 11.8% over one that says referred
    lines that do not            29.4% adopted, 13.1% referred

The distributions are the same, and both lean two to one towards ADOPTED.
"Houses on the Bernal Cut route sold at auction to be removed" sits over
"the Mayor be and he is hereby authorized" — the infinitive describes what
happens to the houses, not what happened to the motion.

So do not read stage out of tense, mood or an infinitive. "Street levels to be
changed" and "street levels changed" are the same fact written twice, and
pairing them as propose/adopt halves one of them on nothing.

Stage comes only from words that NAME a stage: proposed, sought, urged,
requested, referred, sent to committee, continued, put off, tabled, laid over,
passed for second reading, declaring the intention. If none of those is
present, the record is not a proposal — leave the score alone.

## Stage is applied last, and the band follows the number

Choose the band on the decision as if it had happened. Then apply the stage
discount. The record reports whichever band the final number falls in — so a
routine proposal in the 55–79 band lands in 30–54 and is reported there. This
is the one thing allowed to move a record between bands, because a proposal
genuinely is a smaller fact than the act it proposes.

## Wide reach, small stakes

Reach picks the band, and some decisions reach everybody while barely mattering:
bathers must wear a bathing dress; companies must mark the curb where they lay
pipes. A whole class is bound, and nothing much changes.

Those sit at the FOOT of the band their reach earns, not the top — 55-62 rather
than 80. Reach still picks the band, because a universal obligation is genuinely
not a one-address permit; but a band is a range and this is what its bottom is
for.

## Building a thing, and keeping it running

The maintenance note draws a line and this says where it falls. Building or
buying the facility is the facility: an engine house, a branch library, a school
building, the site under it. Keeping it running is upkeep: a roof, a boiler, one
wing, one cistern, the year's gas bill, the water for the hydrants.

The first is scored on what gets built. The second is scored on what is
repaired, however large the institution around it.

THE RECURRING BILL IS UPKEEP. Two readers found this section and the one above
both claiming the same object and answering about thirty points apart — the
hydrant water bill was named as a wide-reach obligation there and as upkeep
here. It is upkeep. The month's street lighting, the year's gas, the water in
the hydrants: the city is keeping a thing running that it already decided to
have, and the deciding is not happening again.

    the month's street-lighting bill                            36
    a bill for one facility — coal for the hospital, its meat   30

The wide-reach section is for a rule BINDING a whole class, not for the invoice
that keeps a service the class already has.

### Four fixes to anchors that were wrong

**The thoroughfare rung was inverted.** It required a listed street AND a named
length, so "Street levels changed along the Great Highway" scored 34 while a
named stretch of that highway scored 40 — the whole below its own part. Three
readers found it firing on 0.3% of lines or never. **Drop the length
requirement: a listed street as the subject is 40.**

**And the street list needs its suffixes.** Nineteenth Avenue is a thoroughfare;
Nineteenth Street is a Potrero side street. Third, Ocean and Nineteenth all
carry the trap. Third, Mission, Market, Van Ness, Geary and Folsom are STREETS —
Third Street carries the rail line; Third Avenue is a Richmond side street.
Ocean and Nineteenth are the AVENUES. Where the name carries both and the line
does not say which, take the street.

**The component anchor had no scale.** The same 45 went to "$25,000 for
engineering on the Hetch Hetchy project" — a plan for the city's whole water
supply — and "$305 for pipes and doors at Polytechnic High School". A component
still takes 45, but a component of a CITYWIDE system takes the system's score
less a third — **except that this rule raises a score and never carries a small
purchase above what the same sum would earn on a single building. Where the sum
is what the line is mostly about, score the sum.** Without that bound "$36 more
toward a Heisler locomotive" took 53 and outranked "$29,311.05 for San Francisco
Hospital brickwork" at 45. The same bound cuts the other way: "$693.01 for Silver
Terrace Playground" must not inherit the playground's 65, and $28.4M of emergency
repairs at Laguna Honda must not be pinned to 36 by an upkeep rule written
against a $325 roof.

**Land for a firehouse outranked the firehouse.** The building anchors split 78
from 68; the land anchor named only 70 and no split. Land is now 62 under a
fire or police station, below the 68 building it sits under.

### Which streets are major

The street-level anchor splits 34 from 40 on whether the street is a major
thoroughfare, and a reader pointed out the rubric named the distinction without
naming the streets — so it wrote its own list and applied it mechanically. That
list is now the rule, because a shared wrong list beats eight private right ones:

    Market, Mission, Geary, Van Ness, Army, Fulton, California, Bush, Sutter,
    Polk, Howard, Folsom, Harrison, Bryant, Dolores, Divisadero, Masonic,
    Columbus, Broadway, Kearny, Montgomery, Sansome, Battery, Third, San Bruno,
    Ocean, Great Highway, Sloat, Portola, Nineteenth, Point Lobos

Where a listed thoroughfare is the SUBJECT of an act on the street itself — its
level, its width, its paving, its sidewalks, its use — the act takes its anchor
plus 6. A length is never required. This raises the act; it does not change what
the act is. An act that merely CROSSES a listed street takes its plain anchor.

This is stated here once and governs every street anchor. It was previously
written into the street-levels anchor alone, which left paving flat at 38 and
sidewalk width flat at 40 — so "Mission Street repaved from Fourth to the
Embarcadero" tied a fifty-foot patch on an alley, and "Van Ness Avenue sidewalks
set at sixteen feet" tied "San Carlos Street sidewalks set at six feet". Eight
readers reported the older wording as self-contradictory.

### The part and the whole

Four readers met the same wall — a civic building going up, paid for in
instalments — and invented four different ladders. A fifth put it exactly:
the gap is not a matter of sums, it is **named part against named whole**.

    Elmer Carlson paid $23,423 for building the Oriental School      the school
    Contra Costa Construction paid $6,913.87 for the Library foundation   a part

So: the whole named facility takes the facility's score. A trade, a component,
a material, a piece of equipment, a plan or a boring takes 45 — it is money at a
site, and the building is not being decided again. Upkeep of a facility already
standing stays where the maintenance rule puts it.

### Three things the discount does badly

**It must not push an anchor through the floor.** "Caldwell & Co. asks for
sixteen more days at Fort Mason" halves the 18 anchor to 9 — which says the
asking is procedure with no subject. It is not. **A discount never takes an act below 18.** Anchors discount like anything else;
the floor catches them. The older rule protected anchors valued below 30 and
exposed everything above, which inverted the scale: a requested 33 permit fell to
17, below the 18 anchor the rule existed to defend; a requested 30 permit fell to
15, below a 22 event permit left untouched; a referred 22 finding scored the same
as an adopted one. Under the single floor a request for any small thing at one
address lands at 18, and no act is ever discounted beneath a smaller one.

**And no act with a named subject falls below 10.** The 0-9 band is for procedure
with no subject in it. A proposal, an appeal, a refusal or a referral is not
that — a settlement, an advisory body, a proclamation and a motion inside an
appeal all have subjects and all stay at 10 or above.

**Discounts do not compound.** Where more than one applies, take the largest
single one.

**A refusal is symmetric only for permits.** The permit row makes refusing and
granting both 33, because a permit refused is a decision about the same small
thing. Stretched further it says "Board turns down a request for the Le Conte
School" is worth 78 — the score of building it — when nothing was built.
**A refusal of anything above the permit tier takes the act at x0.5.**

**"Offers" names a stage.** It was missing from the list, so "City offers Spring
Valley Water Company $87M for all its property" took no discount at all and
topped its batch at 92. An offer is a proposal with a price on it. Add it, with
"declared necessary" and "moves to accept".

### The middle rung of the discount

x0.75 against x0.5 is worth twenty points on a large subject, and readers were
choosing between them with nothing to go on. The test is whether the proposal,
if adopted, would reach past the people directly in front of it:

    Congress urged to ratify the city's reservoir rights of way    x0.75
    a bond election called on acquiring the water supply           x0.75
    W. J. Cuthbertson suggests a city hall at Market and Hermann   x0.5
    departments urged to plan a safer Market Street                x0.5

A proposal by a named private person, or a request to a department to look at
something, is routine. A proposal that would bind a class or change a system is
not.

### A lease is a lease, not a bill

Two readers hit this and one said plainly it was not confident. A lease of space
the public uses is scored on what it houses — that is the rule, and the
recurring-bill rule does NOT reach it. The bill rule is for buying a service
already decided on: the month's gas, the water in the hydrants. Renting the room
a clinic sits in is deciding where the clinic is.

Two of those need saying out loud.

**The land-purchase pair is deliberate, and its gap is now 26 rather than 38.**
The Journal records the same purchase twice, once naming the school it is for
and once naming only a lot and a price. Judging only what the line states is
still right — the reader of the second line genuinely cannot see a school — but
a 38-point gap on one clerk's phrasing was too much. MEASURED: 193 such groups
in the corpus, 0.19% of lines.

**A contractor's extra days is 18, not the street work it attaches to.** It is
one party's business. The street is not being decided again.

**A repeal is not a proposal.** Three readers hit "Order to pave Grant Avenue is
repealed" and had nowhere to put it. Undoing an act is an act, and it reaches
exactly as far as the thing undone — so it takes that score. The Board deciding
NOT to pave a street changed the street as surely as deciding to.

**How far a stage discounts.** Readers were given two levels — half for a
routine proposal, "most" for a significant one — and invented a third for the
middle. There are three:

    passed a committee, passed one reading, recommended     x0.75
    a significant proposal: it would change the city        x0.75
    a routine proposal, a referral, a continuance           x0.5
    a hearing or a question with no proposal pending        x0.5 of the subject

**A department's lease is not money into a facility.** Ninety records a batch
are "the Controller's office extends its lease at 1212 Market Street". The city
housing its own staff is the city's own housekeeping — 26. A lease of space the
PUBLIC uses is the opposite: a clinic, a branch library, a childcare centre, a
shelter. Score that on what it houses.

**A fact recorded is not nothing.** "Engineer Joseph B. Strauss outlines his
Golden Gate bridge plans" and "Sanitary Reduction Works can no longer handle the
city's garbage" are the largest subjects in their batches and neither is an act.
Score the reach of what is reported and take off a third: the Board is telling
the city something true, which is less than doing something, and much more than
adjourning.

## Do not

* Do not score the topic label. Health ranked last of eight topics in one round
  and second of eighteen in another; the label carried nothing.
* Do not reward unusual wording. Measured across a full round: it counts for
  very little, and it promotes charming trivia over consequence.
* Do not reward recency. The year panel shows one year at a time, so records
  compete only against their own year. A recency thumb on the scale would empty
  the historic decades of anything worth reading.
* Do not invent reach the record does not state. If the line says a permit at
  an address, it is an address, however important the building sounds.

## Added in v7

Each rung below was invented independently by three to six readers working
without it. Where they were compared, every reader's numbers landed in the same
band and differed by at most 8 points — so these numbers are not the only right
ones, they are one defensible set, and the point is that everyone uses the same
set. Do not invent your own.

### Traffic and parking regulation

Stop signs, meters, tow-away zones, turn bans, one-way conversions, speed limits,
bike lanes, transit-only lanes, permit-parking districts.

    one street, block or intersection                              36
    a corridor on a listed thoroughfare, or a street made
      one-way, or a turn ban across a stretch                      44
    a district-wide or citywide rule                               58

A street CLOSED keeps its own 36 anchor. These are 30-54 unless the rule is
district-wide, which settles the two sections that claimed them at different
bands.

### A lease of space

    a lease of space the public uses — clinic, branch library,
      childcare, shelter, treatment programme                      58
    court or hearing quarters                                      40
    a department's own office                                      26
    the city leasing a large asset OUT — an airport parcel,
      a concession, a restaurant or cargo lease on city land       48

Score what the space HOUSES. **A line that names only the department scores 26,
even where that department's work is public-facing.** This under-scores real
clinics and intake counters, and it is accepted: the alternative is supplying a
use the record does not state. Six readers met this; five landed on 26, two of
them while saying they disliked the answer. It is settled — do not re-open it.

### Landmark and historic designation

    one building                                                   42
    a district or a group                                          58
    one tree                                                       36

A designation is a permanent rule on what may be done to a thing, not a permit.

### Rezoning

Unlike the ladders above, readers did NOT converge here — the middle rung and the
top both split across bands, because "a stretch", "a block face" and "several
blocks" are not the same thing to different readers. So the rung is DEFINED here,
not merely numbered:

    one lot, one corner, or the parcels of one address             45
    a run of frontage on ONE street, however long                  55
    an area bounded by streets on more than one side, or a
      named neighbourhood                                          68
    a rule on what may be built that binds the whole city          80

### One transit anchor was doing two jobs

The flat 80 covered "ordered, planned or extended", and readers reasonably read
a rerouting as an extension. Measured on the published neighbourhood pages: it
made **46% of every neighbourhood highlight a streetcar or bus record, and 20 of
41 neighbourhoods led with one** — a bus route moved two blocks standing as the
most important thing that ever happened there.

Building the system and adjusting it are not the same act. Opening the Twin
Peaks tunnel changed where the city could live; moving a bus route two blocks
did not. So the anchor splits: a line ordered, planned, built or extended keeps
**80**; a route changed, rerouted, or a stop moved on a line that already runs
takes **55** — a real change a neighbourhood would feel, which is what 55-79 is
for.

This is not a thumb against transit. A new line still outranks almost
everything on the map, and every one of the historic extensions keeps its 80.

### The rule that binds the CITY ITSELF, and the story a paper would run

**This rung was missing, and its absence is measured.** The ladder above and the
80-100 band are both written about LAND and about private conduct — "a rule on
what may be **built**", "an obligation applying to a whole class of people". A
rule about what the CITY may do had no rung at all, so readers fell back to the
neighbourhood rung (68), the ballot rung (75), or wide-reach-small-stakes (55).

Measured over 1,116 records about the city governing itself or about civil
rights: **1 scored 80 or above. 1,046 scored under 55** — while a routine
streetcar route change took a flat 80 from the transit anchor. San Francisco's
surveillance-technology rules, the first of their kind in the United States,
scored 75, 68 and 58 in three different places.

The three rungs, **in this order**, and the order is the point:

    a rule binding what the CITY may do TO PEOPLE — what its police
      may buy or use, what it may collect or keep, who it must or
      must not cooperate with                                       88
    a right or a protection created or removed for a class of
      people                                                        85
    a ban, or a first, that applies across the whole city           82

They sit ABOVE the transit anchor and just under the annual budget, which stays
the largest single act the Board takes. The reasoning, and it is Daniel's: what
the government may do to people is the sharpest kind of story; a right is the
next; a prohibition is concrete but often narrower than either. The budget
recurs every year and these do not, but the budget moves everything at once.

**The test behind all three is whether a newspaper would have run it**, and that
is not a vibe — it is answerable from the record's own words. A record is that
kind of record when it does any of these, all of which the line itself states:

  * binds what the CITY's own officers may do;
  * creates, removes or limits a RIGHT or a PROTECTION for a class;
  * BANS or first-permits something across the city;
  * declares an emergency, or ends one.

**A CONTESTED VOTE IS NOT ONE OF THEM, and that was a mistake I made.** I had
scored a veto, an override or a recorded dissent as evidence a record mattered.
It is not: a unanimous ban matters as much as a six-to-five one, and scoring
conflict would rank a bitter fight over a parking permit above a quiet landmark
decision. Drama is not weight. The stage discounts still apply as they always
did — a veto sustained kills the act, and that is scored by the repeal rule.

Two boundaries, so this does not swallow the corpus. **It is not enough to be
about a serious subject** — a $54,290 shelter grant is money into a service and
scores as that, not as a civil-rights act. And **"wide reach, small stakes" still
applies to rules that bind everybody while changing almost nothing** — the
bathing-dress rule stays at the foot of its band. A citywide PROHIBITION is not
that: it stops something that was happening.

### An appeal keeps its subject

An appeal, a rehearing or a continuance is a rung on the ladder, not a subject.
Score what is being appealed, then discount one rung. "Appeal heard over 203
low-income flats" must not tie "Appeal heard over gym locker rooms". Where the
line does not say what is under appeal, it is a line with no subject and scores
as procedure.

### Score what the line establishes, never what it merely permits

"$71M in housing bonds for Mason and Eddy Streets" establishes housing; the
missing unit count changes the precision, not the kind, so the machinery discount
does not fire. "The Health Department extends its lease at 1548 Stockton Street"
does not establish what is in the building — a clinic is possible there, not
stated.

Where the kind is stated, wording cannot lower it. Where the kind is not stated,
do not supply it.

### A line naming a subject but no act

"Board adopts Resolution No. 571-10 for the Transbay Transit Center" names a
large subject and no act. Score the subject one rung below what the smallest
plausible act on it would earn. Do not score it as procedure — the subject is
stated — and do not borrow the act from the line above it.
